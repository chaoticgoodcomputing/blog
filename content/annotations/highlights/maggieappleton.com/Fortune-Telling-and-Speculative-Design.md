## Annotations

[> [!quote] Quote

> I don’t think design engineering means making fancy micro-interactions and posting them on Twitter, despite what some companies I won’t name may have led you to believe.](https://maggieappleton.com/fortune-telling#:~:text=by%20design%20engineer.-,I%20don%E2%80%99t%20think%20design%20engineering,have%20led%20you%20to%20believe.,-I%20don%E2%80%99t%20think)

Oh, lordy. I’ve been out of the Twitter game long enough to’ve forgotten about seeing people showing off entirely impractical micro-interactions. Those types of post are the pre-LLM hype LinkedIn-core engagement bait. I feel like anybody actually in the space of making things knows that those are in the same category of use as… say… flight propulsion on a car that will never actually make it to market.

I feel bad that this was my first note on the post, but I couldn’t let that slide.

Expressing how this type of creativity and freedom can exist in the general sphere of “engineering” is a very underrated take. In a general shift where [writing everything line-by-line feels more accidental than essential](https://blog.chaoticgood.computer/content/no-silver-bullet-1986), I feel like engineers are grasping for what fills in the creative vacuum of code. Thinking holistically like this is one of many different T-shapes that a post-LLM software engineer can begin to spec into.

[> [!quote] Quote
>
> The first was digital gardens. During the COVID lockdown in 2020, I wrote a very long essay about the idea. It was a different way of publishing on the web: blogs could feel perfectionistic, performative and chronological; gardens grew over time, linked ideas together and pushed back against the feed. That idea became a bit of a phenomenon, peaking around 2021 and 2022.](https://maggieappleton.com/fortune-telling#:~:text=a%20few%20things.-,The%20first%20was%20digital%20gardens.,peaking%20around%202021%20and%202022.,-It%20might%20be)

I know digital gardening predated her essay, but it was certainly a jumping-on point for many folks (myself included). However, I think the theory goes a bit further: it has become *incredibly valuable* to both a) get comfortable finding your own voice in your writing, as well as b) have a written-down record of your own thoughts, even if they aren’t the most polished thing in the world.

On having a record – and this is advice I give often: technical interviews [have always been a bit silly](https://blog.chaoticgood.computer/content/annotations/valve-handbook), but LLMs really did expose just *how* silly they can be. I’ve noticed a general trend of even junior engineering interviews becoming “higher-order” (design this system) over your classic LeetCode problems.

It’s really difficult to learn how these higher-order structures work without just *making* things – over, and over, and over again – so keeping a running record of “here’s what I’ve been up to, here’s how it’s going, and here’s what I’ve learned” is a great way to demonstrate not just *what* you know, but your experience with coming to know it.

[> [!quote] Quote
>
> I predicted an explosion in personal software as models made more people capable of building things, and a rise in local developers who could make software for their communities. Even after the talk, people told me the models wouldn’t be good enough. Now it looks like that prediction is coming to pass.](https://maggieappleton.com/fortune-telling#:~:text=make%20production%20software.-,I%20predicted%20an%20explosion%20in,prediction%20is%20coming%20to%20pass.,-That%E2%80%99s%20enough%20credibility.)

I went to a really great presentation by [Alex Voss](https://corealization.com/) last weekend called [Can we use AI for better ends?](https://www.meetup.com/boston-code-and-coffee/events/317380952/). I think a good framework on which to think about the question is: what is possible now, that wasn’t before?

One of the pieces of data that I’ve personally seen, in [my own work](https://blog.chaoticgood.computer/tags/projects/dayjob), is that there’s been a rise in both:

a) the number of US counties that have websites; and b) the number of US county websites that adhere to [ADA guidelines for website accessibility](https://www.ada.gov/resources/web-guidance/).

It’s pretty obvious that some of these sites had a bit of a(i)ssistance for their frontends. None of their actual *data* was hallucinated, but, y’know… sometimes, you can tell. I wouldn’t say this is any different than the previous trend of, say, Squarespace-assisted web development, though.

Ultimately, the fact that the barrier of entry for creating something online can be a positive thing. More folks than ever, and more folks who need accessibility tools like screenreaders, can now access their community’s information and resources.

I’d call that a pretty solid upside.

I can’t believe this is the first I’m hearing of the formalized [“not invented here”](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Not_invented_here).

This has almost certainly gotten worse in the last 12 months, with the rise of decompilation/“clean room” re-ideation. I’ve seen it a lot on an interpersonal level, even: the whispers of “why would I adapt to this tool, when I could make a bespoke one myself?”
