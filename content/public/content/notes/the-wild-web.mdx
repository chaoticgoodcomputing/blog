## The Eras of the Web

If you've never heard the term Web 1.0 before, it's important that you know where we're coming from.

There was a period of time when websites were *static*: when you were on a website, there was not a whole lot that you, *the user*, could do to change the contents of the page for other people.

The quintessential archive example of this concept is the [website for the 1996 *Space Jam* movie](https://www.spacejam.com/1996/). You, the user, aren't a factor in the website: there's no login, nowhere to *like and subscribe*. You can click through to see Space Jam news, get coloring book printouts, and when you leave, there's no trace you were ever there.[^3]

*That* is Web 1.0.

Web 2.0 is the era that came after that. Funnily enough, my old Geocities site is a natural bridge, with its hyperlink to my [Roblox account](https://web.archive.org/web/20090712000801/http://www.roblox.com/User.aspx?ID=119813)[^4]. In a strange way, [[public/tags/projects/games/roblox|Roblox]] was like Baby's First Web 2.0 experience. In its original [[public/content/articles/robloxaville#2. Monetization|pre-monetized form]], the site was a textbook example of *user-generated content*. Even though I was making games, Roblox wasn't *my* site. I didn't own it. Instead, my *content* was being uploaded and served out of their servers.

That was the start of an era where websites were no longer just static information. They were places where you could comment and subscribe. You could place an order, or poke your friends -- you know, back before Facebook's business model was metaverse avatars and advertising eating disorders to teenagers.

Web 2.0 is[^5] the commercialization and consolidation of the internet into a core set of applications and platforms that everybody builds on top of.

## Social Networks, not Social Media

Like many older members of Gen Z, I spent my fair share of the 2010s with social media accounts. Facebook, for keeping up with older family members. Twitter[^6], for shitposting with friends. I'll even admit to using Snapchat, back when we were all too dumb and naive to think that any of that content *actually* self-destructed.

Of course, between swinging elections, being bought out by billionaires, and generally developing into a thin facade for shilling either bad products or even worse ideas, I largely deleted all of my social media at the turn of the decade.

The only two I have left are LinkedIn -- which feels more like a professional courtesy, kept [strictly at arms length](https://bsky.app/profile/speen.us/post/3mz7l5i7f322l) -- and Bluesky.

Bluesky was an interesting case. I can't say that I even used Twitter all that much anymore before it was purchased, but I did stay up-to-date on Bluesky's launch and initial growth because I was interested in the promise of [ATProto](https://atproto.com/), which creates a social network environment a bit more like old-school email.[^7]

I've certainly outgrown the 2010s dream of becoming Twitter Famous™, but I have thought more about the concept of social networks since picking up my [[public/content/season-of-stamps|Season of Stamps]]. Part of Stamps is to be more social, building new relationships after moving across the country. Admittedly, the last ~3 months since moving have been pretty lonely[^8], and social media is, if nothing else, *social*.

It's in that state of mind that I noticed something interesting, recently. I read through [an interesting presentation about Boundary Objects](https://maggieappleton.com/planning-agents) and caught a section at the very end of the post:

> **Mentions around the web**

It *looks* like a comment section, but it isn't a comment: it's another blog that *mentions* her blog post, presumably with a link. I pulled on that thread a bit, and that's when I found something *wild*: [IndieWeb](https://indieweb.org), [WebMentions](https://webmention.net/), and a social network that's practically invisible unless you know what to look for.

## IndieWeb, as described by an IndieNewb

So, as I understand it, this is how WebMentions work:

1. I publish a blog post with a hyperlink[^9]
2. I send a request via some service like [`webmention.io`](https://webmention.io/) to the site I linked to
3. The owner of the site receives a notification that `blog.chaoticgood.computer` mentioned them
4. They can choose to include that mention on *their* site, in whatever form factor they'd like.

This is a sneaky little process, isn't it? There's not really a *platform* to speak of: there's not a single feed anywhere to see any of this. It's just a casual "Hey, I mentioned you!", and a choice to acknowledge the mention.

The tricky part: it's *elegant*, but *invisible*. I'm the type of person who gets into this kind of thing, and [[public/content/notes/digital-gardening-with-quartz|I've been reading Maggie Appleton's blog for awhile now]] without ever noticing that there was some under-the-hood magic going on.

In my mind, something that's hard to see is also hard to understand, and something that's hard to understand is hard to jump into.

So, let's try to see it.

## The webs of the Web

Since learning about this, I've gone ahead and updated this site to support WebMentions:

1. Outgoing mentions are handled by a [GitHub Action](https://github.com/chaoticgoodcomputing/blog/actions/runs/41875712182/job/126735885721) that triggers after I push out new content
2. Incoming mentions are handled through [`webmentions.io`](https://webmention.io/)[^10]

While my scrapes of Jimmy or Birgitta's sites didn't yield much[^12], Maggie's blog is a real hotspot of WebMention activity.

Now that we're scraping outside of our own domain, I've added a bit of extra sauce. First, we can now see incoming and outgoing mentions for those sites: not just who is mentioning them, but who *they* are mentioning.

Second: I've also stolen a bit of a Twitter-ism -- the idea of the *mutual*. On follow-based platforms, mutuals are somebody who you follow, and who follows you back. Here, I've highlighted mutuals specifically, with the hope to find not just chatter, but actual back-and-forth conversations happening on the IndieWeb within my immediate vicinity.

Finally, I've highlighted an important finding: [IndieWeb itself](https://news.indieweb.org/en). You're going to see this one come up a lot more as the graphs progress. IndieWeb allows folks to *mention* IndieWeb in order to consolidate posts *about* IndieWeb. It's a real turducken of self-reference. Compliments to the chefs: it's a beautiful thing to see.
